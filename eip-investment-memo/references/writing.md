# Writing the memo

The memo is an argument, and its reader is deciding whether to commit capital. That
reader is experienced, short on time, and reading several memos this month. Everything
below follows from that.

## Evidence discipline

**Every number traces to an input.** If a figure is needed and you don't have it, write
`[TBD — source]` and list it in the diligence gaps. Do not interpolate, do not carry a
plausible number forward from a similar deal, and do not compute a derived metric from
two numbers that came from different periods or different definitions.

The reason is not pedantry. A fabricated figure in an approval memo is formatted exactly
like a diligenced one, so nobody catches it, and it can be relied on in a funding
decision. This is the one failure mode of this document that actually costs money.
Finishing every slide matters less than this.

**Distinguish the source's authority.** These are different claims and should read
differently:

| Phrasing | Means |
|---|---|
| "Per the CBIZ QofE" | Third-party diligenced |
| "Management reports" | Asserted, not verified |
| "Per the sponsor model" | Sponsor's forecast, not EIP's view |
| "EIP analysis" | Our own derivation from underlying data |

A reader who cannot tell diligenced fact from management assertion cannot calibrate the
risk, and the memo has failed at its job.

**Keep the adjustment bridge visible.** Adjusted EBITDA is the number the whole
transaction is priced off. Show reported-to-adjusted with each adjustment named,
quantified, and attributed. An adjusted figure presented without its bridge is the most
common weak point in a memo and the first thing IC will push on.

**Cite interim and stub periods precisely.** "LTM Feb-26" and "FY25" are not
interchangeable, and a YTD figure compared against a full-year budget is a mistake that
looks like a finding.

## Titles

Every content slide carries a two-tone title: bold green subject, grey descriptive half.

The grey half states the finding. This is the highest-leverage writing in the deck —
someone flipping through reads only titles, and a deck whose titles state conclusions
can be understood at that speed.

| Weak | Better |
|---|---|
| Pros & Cons | Pros & Cons — Risks Mitigated by Long-Dated MSAs and a Tenured Field Workforce |
| Top Customers | Top Customers — Concentration Offset by Nine-Year Average Tenure and Full Renewal History |
| Backlog Trends | Backlog Trends — Four Consecutive Quarters of Award-Driven Growth |
| Financial Forecast | Financial Forecast — EIP Downside Case Holds 1.4x Fixed Charge Coverage |

If you cannot write a grey half that says something, the slide has no finding yet.
Either find it in the inputs or reconsider whether the slide belongs.

Exceptions: the cover, section dividers, and pure-reference appendix exhibits where the
title is a label rather than a claim.

## Prose

**Lead with the label.** `**Label**: explanation`. The label carries the claim, the body
the evidence. A reader skimming only bold text should still follow the argument.

**One idea per paragraph.** Memo paragraphs run two to four lines. Longer and they stop
being skimmable; the commentary rail is where a longer reading belongs.

**Quantify.** "Strong customer retention" is not a finding. "Nine-year average tenure
across the top three customers, all renewed at least once" is.

**Write in the firm's voice, not the sponsor's.** Sponsor materials are promotional and
their language carries assumptions. Restate claims in neutral terms and attribute them.
"Transformational platform opportunity" becomes "sponsor intends to pursue add-on
acquisitions; two targets identified, neither under LOI."

**Prefer the concrete.** Name the customer, the covenant level, the month. Vagueness in
a memo usually means the underlying fact wasn't nailed down, and it is better to expose
that than to paper over it.

## Section-by-section notes

**Opportunity Summary.** The densest slide in the memo, and often the only one some
readers finish. Cover: what the sponsor is doing and at what price and multiple; what
the company is; how the transaction is financed; who else is in the equity; what EIP's
position is; why it fits EIP's mandate. Written well it stands alone.

**Updates Since Preliminary Approval.** Lead with the prior IC votes and date, then what
diligence closed, then what changed. Changes that make the deal *worse* go first —
finding them buried is what destroys trust in a memo.

**Pros & Cons.** Three to five items each side. Every risk gets a real mitigant or an
explicit "no mitigant identified." A risks column made of strawmen ("integration risk,"
mitigated by "experienced management") is transparent to an IC and costs the whole memo
its credibility. The strongest version names the risk that genuinely worries the deal
team.

**Call summaries.** Report what the reference actually said, including hesitation. A
page of uniformly glowing references reads as selection bias. Where a call raised a
concern, say so and note whether it was resolved.

**Forecast cases.** The narrative slide explains what distinguishes each case in terms
of *assumptions* — growth rate, margin path, working capital, capex — not outputs.
Present cases in the same table shape so they compare line by line. The downside case
must be genuinely adverse: one that still comfortably clears covenants hasn't tested
anything, and the covenant headroom under that case is what the credit decision turns on.

**Sponsor overview.** Strategy, relevant track record, and what they intend to do with
this asset. For a first-time or emerging fund, say so plainly and address it in the
risks — it is a real consideration and IC will raise it regardless.

**Diligence gaps.** Name every open item, who owns it, and when it closes. This section
makes the memo more credible, not less. An IC that sees a well-specified open item trusts
the closed ones more.
